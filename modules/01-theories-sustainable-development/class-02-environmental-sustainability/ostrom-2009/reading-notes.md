# Ostrom: conditional cooperation in commons dilemmas

Assigned chapter: 2009, pp. 207–228. Supplied document: 2008 working paper W08–23, 39 PDF pages. All present locators refer to that working paper. The chapter and working paper have not been established as equivalent.

## Argument and interpretation

Ostrom examines variation in cooperation rather than treating shared resources as destined for depletion. The inspected discussion of anonymous, noncommunicating experimental settings supports a conditional finding: those conditions can produce overharvesting. It cannot establish that people always fail to cooperate around a common resource (O-01, supplied working paper PDF page 2 and conclusion pages 25–26).

Monitoring and graduated sanctions matter partly because participants can observe whether others adhere to rules. That makes reciprocity more plausible and helps explain why institutional arrangements can change interaction (O-02, page 25). The claim is conditional. A monitoring form does not show that monitoring occurred, that a violation was detected, or that sanctions were feasible.

Ecological conditions, social relationships, institutional rules, and diverse motivations interact (O-03, page 26 and figures pages 37–39). This directs the analyst toward a mechanism in context. It does not provide a universal recipe based on community size or one preferred form of ownership. Nora's warnings about policy transfer add a practical reason to examine local actors and incentives (L2-02).

An electricity grid, a watershed, atmospheric capacity, and a privately owned data center are different objects. Calling all of them “commons” would obscure access rules, rivalry, exclusion, ownership, and the geographical boundary of harm. A course application must first specify which shared resource and which interdependence are at issue.

The urban case can then investigate who uses the resource, who sets rules, who observes use, who can act after a violation, and why actors might cooperate repeatedly. Evidence of an agreement is a starting point. A stronger account follows actual operation and tests whether cooperation preserves the relevant physical conditions.

## Conditional cooperation and a falsifiable application

The working paper describes trust and reciprocity as possibilities whose effects depend on context (`O-04`, PDF p. 12). Merely assuming that people can cooperate does not establish that they will. An urban application should therefore identify the information, incentives and repeated interactions that make a response plausible, then search for failures as well as success.
