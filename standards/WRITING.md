# E5 writing and editorial standard

A reader should be able to identify the question, assess the evidence, and understand the limits without reconstructing the author's reasoning. This is the repository's operational application of Mark's E5 materials: *Effective Writing: A Working System*, *Anti-AI Writing System*, *Academic Writing*, and *Voice Register System*, inspected in the E5 project mirror. These rules govern authored prose; quotations retain the source's wording.

## Before writing

Record audience, purpose, decision or question, output length, and the claim the evidence permits. Select only the theories that change the analysis. A short memo needs a coherent inquiry; it cannot serve as an inventory of everything read. A presentation needs an argument an audience can follow once, aloud.

For each substantive paragraph identify its job: establish a fact, explain a mechanism, evaluate an alternative, report personal experience, or identify an uncertainty. Mixed paragraphs must make transitions explicit. Place the main point early, then develop its support. Use ordinary nouns and active verbs. Identify who decides, what changes, and who experiences the consequence.

## Evidence and reasoning

Connect claim → source observation → warrant → qualification. The warrant explains why the observation supports the claim. Distinguish association from attribution, capacity from consumption, a promise from implementation, and an outcome from a causal effect. Cite the passage, record, or dataset that supports the actual sentence. A footnote to an entire report cannot rescue a claim the report does not establish.

State the strongest relevant objection accurately. Explain whether it changes the conclusion, reduces confidence, or remains unresolved. Specify what evidence would change the assessment. Do not decorate an unsupported claim with a numerical probability. Use confidence language with a reason, such as limited by one interested source or by uncertain facility boundaries.

Personal reflection may express a lived experience or a value without an external citation. Empirical generalizations drawn from that experience require evidence. Do not invent an epiphany or present an AI-authored introduction as Mark's testimony.

## Editorial passes

1. Check the assignment, audience, and purpose.
2. State the central question and bounded answer.
3. Put each paragraph in the order needed by the reader.
4. Remove facts that do not advance the inquiry.
5. Check every external claim against its exact source.
6. Identify the inference connecting evidence to conclusion.
7. Test assumptions, alternatives, and counterevidence.
8. Separate facts, values, experience, and uncertainty.
9. Replace abstractions with actors, actions, and consequences.
10. Remove repetition, inflated praise, and promotional vocabulary.
11. Remove em dashes, rhetorical contrasts, and question-answer theatrics from authored prose.
12. Read aloud for connected sentences and natural transitions.
13. Check references, units, figures, word count or speaking time.
14. Have Mark verify the argument and personal voice before submission.

Prefer connected prose. Use tables for comparisons and lists for genuinely parallel or sequential information. Avoid bold-titled bullets, decorative headings, generic conclusions, and claims of being elite or rigorous without evidence. Research quality is demonstrated by the work.

## Acceptance examples

Weak: “The project is sustainable because it uses efficient technology.”

Precise research question: “Did the efficiency improvement reduce annual electricity consumption once workload growth was included?”

Weak: “The city had strong governance.”

Precise research question: “Which authority could enforce the operating condition, and what record shows that it exercised that authority?”

These examples are editorial exercises, not case findings. A prose linter can flag patterns; it cannot assess truth, voice, or scholarly contribution. Human review remains necessary.
