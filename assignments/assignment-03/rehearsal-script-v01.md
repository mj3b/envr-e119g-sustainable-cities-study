## 1. The problem

A developer proposes a cooling redesign that would use less groundwater. What can a public authority conclude from that proposal?

It might be an important technical improvement. But a forecast of lower water demand, permission to change a project, and evidence of environmental benefit answer different questions. My study examines how those distinctions mattered in the environmental review of the Cerrillos Data Center in Chile.

I began with a broader concern about accountability for water commitments. I have narrowed it to one documented episode between 2020 and 2024: how the environmental tribunal treated a proposed redesign alongside the project's existing approval.

## 2. What happened in the records

In its 2024 judgment, the court described a proposal to replace water-based cooling towers with air-cooled equipment. It reported that an administrative screening had found that the proposed change did not require a new entry into environmental assessment.

That sounds decisive, but the type of decision matters. The screening answered a question about entry into assessment. The court distinguished that answer from changing the original environmental authorization. The agency's guidance likewise states that a screening response does not amend that authorization or itself grant permission to execute a project.

The tribunal continued examining the original groundwater assessment. It partially annulled the approval, required reconsideration addressing climate change, and suspended the remaining effects pending a complementary authorization. A later resolution acknowledged the project holder's renunciation and declared that the project could not proceed under the original terms.

These records establish legal treatment. They do not measure water savings.

## 3. Why this belongs in sustainable cities

North helps me ask which rules and authorities shape a decision, and what evidence would show enforcement. Satterthwaite adds a different question: does an apparent environmental improvement transfer costs to other people, ecosystems, or future periods?

Together, they prevent me from treating good paperwork as sufficient proof of sustainability, or lower projected groundwater demand as a complete environmental assessment.

The Amazonia volume edited by Professor Libertun de Duren reinforces attention to ecological functions, people, and reliable local data. I use its particular contributions to refine my questions. I am not treating a Chilean cooling system as an Amazonian nature-based intervention.

## 4. How I am investigating it

My method is a documentary case study. For each material statement, I record who issued it, which document version contains it, where it appears, and what kind of claim it supports.

A crucial limit is that several original technical and administrative records remain uninspected. When the court describes those records, I attribute the description to the court. I cannot use it to reconstruct equipment quantities or calculate annual water savings.

I also retain rival explanations. Ordinary screening and judicial review may have worked as intended. Technical or commercial reasons could help explain the redesign. The order of events alone cannot decide among those explanations.

## 5. What the study can contribute

The study aims to explain what must be checked before an environmental-improvement claim becomes a basis for public action. It does not establish implemented savings, aquifer recovery, or agreement among all affected people.

Cerrillos is a computing-infrastructure case, not evidence that AI workloads caused the dispute. AI assisted my research organization and this script; I remain responsible for reviewing the argument.

The practical question is: which commitment governs, who can act on it, and what evidence would establish the consequences?
