The Cerrillos Data Center case in Chile taught me that a project’s approval tells only part of the story about residents’ water concerns. A participation request was ruled late; the concern later received an agency response; and the court subsequently required reassessment. Following that sequence narrowed my earlier question about accountability for water commitments to data centers. I could investigate how a concern was received, answered and reconsidered while keeping outcomes on the ground an open question.

The central lesson is that a resident needs a way to connect a question to the authority’s answer and any later decision. That connection is difficult to reconstruct when each document tells only part of the story. I propose a public response register: a plain-language index showing the concern, official response, responsible office and evidence still needed. Its usefulness would need testing with residents.

## 1. How my question became more focused

My earlier inquiry considered a broad chain of justification, disclosure, monitoring, responsibility and remedies. It already recognized that approving a project does not establish its benefits. What I lacked was a manageable way to investigate how those connections worked in practice. Cerrillos offered records through which I could follow one concern. It also shifted the institutional setting from municipal water commitments to environmental approval.

The 2019 filing stood out to me. It made me ask what happens to residents’ water concerns after the agency rules their request late. The applicant questioned groundwater use and asked Chile’s Environmental Assessment Service (SEA) to open formal public participation. SEA recorded receipt on August 19 and rejected the request against its August 16 deadline (Cerrillos participation applicants, 2019, p. 2; SEA, 2019a, p. 1).

That ruling settled whether this request could open formal participation. Following the concern further became my next task. This narrower approach lets me examine an actual sequence of responses before attempting to assess the entire project’s effects. It also brings the social dimension into the inquiry through a documented resident concern. The filing alone cannot show how other residents understood the project or whose views were absent.

## 2. What following the concern revealed

The documents gave me three findings to work with. First, the request’s procedural status and the later treatment of the water concern needed separate examination. The original approval says there was no request in its account of formal participation, while the assessment report records a request rejected as late (Comisión de Evaluación Ambiental de la Región Metropolitana, 2020, consideration 11, p. 42; SEA, 2020a, pp. 88–89). A reader consulting only the approval could miss that history. I cannot establish why the wording differs.

Second, the concern later received a substantive answer. Residents asked SEA to set the approval aside. SEA addressed groundwater modeling, monthly monitoring and corrective measures before rejecting that challenge (Cerrillos resident applicants, 2020, pp. 10, 16–17; SEA, 2020b, pp. 21–23, 35). The earlier timing ruling therefore does not establish that the water concern went unanswered.

Third, an answer remained open to scrutiny. The tribunal subsequently found that significant aquifer effects had not been adequately ruled out and that monitoring could not substitute for adequate prior assessment. It partially annulled the approval and suspended the remainder pending reconsideration. Reassessment was to consider climate effects on groundwater where applicable, taking account of a possible cooling change (Segundo Tribunal Ambiental, 2024, considerations 98–101 and operative provisions 1–2, pp. 70–72).

**Figure 1. The documented sequence for the original approval**

2019 · Participation request ruled late → 2020 · SEA answers water concerns; challenge rejected → February 2024 · Tribunal requires reassessment → Later 2024 · Commission acknowledges the developer giving up the original approval

Sources: SEA (2019a, p. 1; 2020b, pp. 21–23, 35), Segundo Tribunal Ambiental (2024, pp. 70–72), and Comisión de Evaluación Ambiental de la Región Metropolitana (2024, p. 3). Sequence alone does not establish residents’ influence on redesign or renunciation.

Later in 2024, the Commission acknowledged the developer’s renunciation of the original approval, so it could no longer authorize the project (Comisión de Evaluación Ambiental de la Región Metropolitana, 2024, p. 3). This limits what I can claim about that approval’s outcomes. The selected records establish a concern, an agency response and subsequent review. They do not establish installed cooling changes, measured water savings or improved service for nearby users. Those findings require their own evidence.

## 3. What the course readings help me understand

Sen provides the main concept guiding my interpretation. His account of human development gives informed public discussion a role in judging the dimensions of development and their relative importance (2000, p. 21). Applied here, the question is whose water outcomes enter the assessment and how affected people can question those choices. A facility’s water demand and a household’s service reliability answer different questions. Residents need to see which outcomes officials considered before they can challenge an omission or disagree with the reasons.

The Rio upgrading study supplies a practical methodological lesson. Libertun de Duren et al. (2021, PDF pp. 4–5, 7–9) combined infrastructure inspections with focus groups in nine communities. For my inquiry, this suggests examining physical conditions and residents’ experiences through evidence suited to each. Equipment records could establish installation; meters could record withdrawals; residents could identify service interruptions needing investigation. Rio’s findings cannot establish conditions in Cerrillos, but its approach helps me design the next stage.

Together, these readings make the sustainability question more specific: what changed, for whom, and who can question the assessment? A public record should make those questions visible. It should also identify assumptions that may need reconsideration as water conditions change and the institution responsible for responding. Whether this improves decisions is an empirical question.

## 4. How a city could apply this learning

The practical proposal follows directly from the difficulty I encountered: create one traceable entry for each concern within a published scope. The environmental authority would maintain links to official decisions, subject to its mandate and capacity. Municipal staff could help residents locate entries and request corrections. The register would preserve original documents and existing review routes.

**Table 1. An illustrative register entry using the reviewed case**

| Entry field | Information the reader would find |
| --- | --- |
| Concern | Groundwater use questioned in the 2019 filing. |
| Response and later review | SEA’s response on modeling and monitoring, followed by the tribunal’s reassessment requirement. |
| Responsible body | SEA issued the response; the tribunal ordered reassessment. Responsibility for implementation needs a separate check. |
| Follow-through | Original approval relinquished. The selected records do not establish installed changes or neighborhood water benefits. |

Proposed format using the records cited in Section 2. No custodian has been appointed and no public service has been implemented.

Plain-language explanations, paper access and a correction route could help make the proposal usable beyond technically confident readers. Public entries would omit contact details of applicants. Any water-use figure would identify its period and operating conditions. A claimed benefit would identify the users and outcomes covered. These requirements could help residents and officials locate a missing explanation or evidence of an unmet obligation.

If a later measurement challenges an assumption in an approval, an entry should identify the office responsible for assessing that evidence and record its response. This connects clearer documentation to potential environmental action. The register would still depend on staff capacity, legal authority and measurements. An outdated entry could mislead readers, so the trial should test whether maintaining it is feasible.

I would begin with a small volunteer trial comparing the existing case file with the proposed entry. Participants would locate an answer, identify the responsible office and explain what remains unknown. I would record completion, search time, errors and assistance, while staff recorded maintenance effort. Recruitment limits and unsuccessful attempts would be reported. This trial has not occurred; the completed work is documentary research with AI-assisted claim review.

The proposal cannot establish representative participation or environmental improvement. People absent from the filings may have different concerns, and later interviews would need consent and a separate plan. Its immediate purpose is modest: help people follow a concern and identify who can answer the unresolved question. That is the practical step my research now supports investigating. Its contribution to sustainable and resilient cities would depend on whether clearer public scrutiny leads institutions to revisit assumptions and act when water conditions require it.
