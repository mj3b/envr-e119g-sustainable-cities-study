## Priority reading route before Assignment 2

### 1. Assigned Nature-Based Solutions section

**Gauto Espinola and Llaguno Ribadeneira; printed pp. 157–161 / PDF 158–162.**

The section is organized around ecological functions rather than a single generic green intervention. Its five entries address absorption, evapotranspiration and shade, wastewater treatment, biodiversity and connectivity, and cultural-ecological relations. Read each as a proposed mechanism with a setting, beneficiaries and implementation conditions. The final discussion concerns zoning, institutions and local ecological knowledge.

Ask what the proposed action actually changes: runoff, temperature, pollutant removal, habitat connections or relationships between a community and a landscape. These are different phenomena. A common sustainability label does not make their evidence interchangeable.

For the Cerrillos memo, use the section to refine evaluation criteria and the explanation of stakes. The water-saving cooling proposal has not been shown to protect or restore ecosystems through a nature-based mechanism. No wetlands, green-infrastructure mandate or measured NbS benefit should be assigned to the case from this reading alone.

### 2. Nora's opening and conclusion

**Nora Libertun de Duren; printed pp. 22–30 and170–174 / PDF 23–31 and171–175.**

The opening identifies fragmentation, hybridity and remoteness as central to the region's urban structure. These concepts explain why territorial histories, ecological conditions and modes of livelihood complicate standardized urban planning. Read the language as a regional diagnosis, not as a ready-made description of Chile.

The conclusion discusses tensions among connectivity and conservation, local needs and global interests, and traditional and modern forms of urbanization. Printed p. 173 is particularly useful: it explicitly identifies data and evaluation gaps despite promising interventions. This supports careful interpretation of environmental promises without implying that the book is hostile to technology or development.

The conclusion also makes strong normative arguments for investment and partnership. Keep those recommendations separate from identified empirical effect estimates. A policy preference is not a comparative causal result merely because it appears beside quantitative analysis.

### 3. Formal responsibilities and operating capacity

**Daniela Torres Peláez, Roberto Arana Fierros and Fernanda Balbino; printed pp. 90–94 / PDF 91–95.**

The section distinguishes devolved mandates from fiscal and institutional capacity. Its table and country discussion address different administrative tiers and geographical populations. Compare definitions before using percentages side by side. In particular, a department, municipality and region are not interchangeable units.

For Assignment 2, the useful distinction is between a legal power and an ability to exercise it. The section supplies a comparative question, not proof of limited capacity in the Chilean authority. Preserve the possibility that ordinary screening and the legal scope of judicial review adequately explain the records.

### 4. Environmental governance and participation

**Marcela Ángel and Marco Herndon, as named in the section heading; printed pp. 151–156 / PDF 152–157.**

The discussion links governance innovations to local contexts, sustainable local financing, community knowledge and institutional partnerships. It includes examples of technologies and public services. Those examples vary in source type and measurement. Treat a described pilot, a recommended practice and a documented operating program as different evidence categories.

Participation should be investigated as a process with identifiable participants, authority, constraints and records. A project description's inclusive language cannot establish representative agreement. Nor can litigation withdrawal alone do that work in Cerrillos.

### 5. Adaptation pathways

**Mariel Juárez Olvera and Luis Mora; printed pp. 144–150 / PDF 145–151.**

The authors discuss defining ecological tipping points, sequencing measures and establishing monitoring and adaptive governance. Figure 4.1 on printed p. 146 / PDF 147 presents an illustrative 2025–2125 flood-management pathway. Its circles mark transfer opportunities and its triangles mark action tipping points. It is an explanation of a planning approach, not observed hundred-year performance.

This is important to the history of our inquiry. Adaptive sequencing and monitoring-linked action are already part of the urban-development repertoire described in the course. They should not be repackaged as an original broad theory of urban correction. The narrower contribution remains reconstruction of what specific documents establish about a specific decision.

### 6. Service-access measures and methods

**Andrés Blanco, Raphaelle Ortiz and Javier Cuervo; printed pp. 95–99. Box 3.2 by Paloma Martín and Pablo Mahnic, printed pp. 97–98. Appendix 2A by Paloma Martín and María de los Ángeles Scetta, printed pp. 184–185.**

The infrastructure discussion distinguishes water availability and dependable household service. Box 3.2 defines one measure through piped-water access and uses mixed census dates. Figures 3.1 and3.2 have different country/year details from the box. None should be turned into a single current and uniformly defined deficit percentage without resolving those differences.

Appendix 2A records an adaptation of urbanization-classification thresholds for Amazonia and a different treatment of non-Amazonian territories. This is useful methodological transparency: the reader can ask whether the classification matches the research question. It is not a reason to assume every resulting comparison is invalid, but it is a reason to preserve the classification choice.

## Three visual objects worth discussing

**Figure 1.1, printed p. 29 / PDF 30:** a conceptual route from history and geography, through urban characteristics and deficits, to proposed strategies. It explains the book's organization. The connecting lines are conceptual relationships, not estimated causal coefficients.

**Figures 3.1–3.2, printed p. 96 / PDF 97:** country comparisons of water and sanitation access with visibly different census years. The figures illustrate why a regional headline needs a defined measure, population and time basis. Reading the date row is as important as reading the tallest bar.

**Figure 4.1, printed p. 146 / PDF 147:** an illustrative adaptation pathway. Treat its dates and action lengths as part of an example, not as a validated engineering forecast. Monitoring and decision points need original implementation records before they can become evidence about an actual project.

## Memo recommendation

Use the assigned section for one carefully located discussion of ecological fit and people. Use Nora's conclusion for the limit on inferring impact from promising innovations. Retain the governance and methods passages as support for the research design. Additional chapter summaries belong in the course record unless the assignment brief requires a broader literature treatment.

The book strengthens the reason to ask the Cerrillos question. It does not answer that question on behalf of the original Chilean instruments.
