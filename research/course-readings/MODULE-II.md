# Air quality: what the readings teach about evidence

[Home](../../README.md) / [Course readings](README.md) / Module II

Module II is useful to the Cerrillos study because its papers separate several questions that an environmental claim can blur: whether a policy exists, whether people or organizations act, whether exposure changes, and whether health or ecological conditions improve. Different research designs answer different parts of that sequence. None of these readings establishes what happened to groundwater at Cerrillos.

This is an AI-assisted, source-bounded research note for human review. It draws on five supplied PDFs and a separately inspected publisher HTML article. It is not a systematic review of all air-quality research, a replication of the papers, or Assignment 2 prose. Exact source versions, selected reading pages and candidate uses appear in the [source cards](source-cards.json). The [quantitative register](quantitative-register.json) retains values, denominators and unresolved discrepancies.

## 1. Blackman and Hoffmann: locate the effect that was actually estimated

Blackman and Hoffmann (2025) study university students in Bogotá who received a package of information about an air-quality app, information intended to motivate its use, and a six-week interactive email campaign. Assignment occurred at the baseline-session level. The paper describes 665 baseline participants, 578 endline participants and 536 complete cases, of whom 226 were in treatment and 310 in control. Those numbers describe different stages of the sample; none can substitute silently for another. The experiment had 30 baseline sessions, not 536 independently randomized assignment units. See PDF p. 5, printed p. 503.

The bounded empirical result concerns reported behavior. In Table 4, Panel B, the coefficient for any avoidance behavior is 0.092. For a binary outcome, multiplying by 100 expresses the estimate as a **9.2 percentage-point increase**, with conventional p = 0.008 and false-discovery-rate q = 0.021. The mask and window-closing estimates are 4.5 and 7 percentage points, respectively. The paper's table stars correspond to the adjusted q-values, so a star cannot be read as the corresponding conventional p-value. These are estimates for the bundled intervention and the measured survey responses. They are not estimates of an isolated app effect, emitted pollution, personal inhaled dose or observed mortality. See PDF p. 13, printed p. 511.

The design also matters for interpreting mechanism. The authors cannot disentangle the three treatment components because they used one bundled treatment arm. The information-to-behavior pathway is an explanatory structure; an observed response to the package does not establish each intermediate causal link. The authors acknowledge self-reporting bias, overlap with the COVID-19 pandemic and uncertainty about generalizing from students to other groups. They call for replications with observable behavior and other populations. See PDF pp. 17–18, printed pp. 515–516.

The separate health-benefit section uses epidemiological simulation and benefit transfer. It introduces assumptions about scale-up, sustained behavior and mask performance. Those consequences were not measured by the trial. Moreover, the supplied PDF contains unresolved numerical problems in that section. On printed p. 514, the middle scenario is described as a 64.3% reduction from 10.5 baseline deaths to 6.7 post-intervention deaths. The change implied by those two printed values is approximately 36.2%. That arithmetic check identifies a discrepancy; it does not establish whether 6.7 should instead denote avoided deaths or what the intended estimate was. The page also prints scenario thresholds in mg/m³. The page image confirms that unit; it has not been silently replaced.

The proper response is selective. Retain the useful trial design and reported-behavior results with their limitations. Block the mortality and monetary figures from reuse as verified evidence until the appendix and original calculations or a clarification resolve the discrepancy. This is neither a finding of misconduct nor a rejection of the whole paper.

**Application to Assignment 2:** distinguish the proposed cooling design, its projected resource effect, its legal treatment, any implementation evidence and any measured environmental effect. Blackman and Hoffmann supply a methodological comparison about inference boundaries. Their effect sizes cannot estimate anything about Cerrillos.

## 2. Chakraborty and colleagues: the environmental average is not the distribution

Chakraborty and colleagues (2022) ask who lives in places with higher modeled outdoor PM2.5. Their two analyses use different units: 61,117 eligible census tracts in counties with public housing, and 6,685 developments with the required complete demographic records. The exposure estimate combines modeled annual concentrations from 2011–2015; the housing records are from 2015. At development level, the assigned concentration comes from the census block group containing the development's representative location. It is not a personal exposure monitor. See PDF pp. 6–8.

The descriptive comparison makes the distribution visible. Approximately 35% of public-housing residents, compared with 24% of the general population, were located in the highest PM2.5 tract quartile. The paper then uses multivariable generalized estimating equations to examine associations while accounting for specified demographic variables and clustering. Both the descriptive shares and the adjusted associations are useful within their stated populations. Neither identifies a causal siting process or an individual health effect. See Figure 2 and Table 1, PDF p. 3.

Coverage limits are part of the finding. The paper excludes voucher-based rental assistance, does not study indoor air quality, and does not identify the pollution sources responsible for the modeled concentrations. Suppressed counts for 311 of 7,000 geocoded developments were replaced by 1 for the tract analysis, a procedure the authors describe as likely to undercount units and residents. The methods also contain a specification discrepancy: Table 2 describes an unstructured correlation matrix, whereas the methods describe an independent matrix for Phase 1. That difference remains unresolved rather than being harmonized by an editor. See PDF pp. 4 and 6–8.

**Application to Assignment 2:** ask whether the evidence identifies affected populations and which populations it excludes. A litigation participant list cannot stand in for the surrounding population, just as one spatial measure cannot stand in for individual exposure. That is a research-design caution. It does not establish unequal exposure, exclusion or harm in Cerrillos without local evidence.

## 3. Gu and colleagues: index construction changes the question

The supplied Gu manuscript examines city-level associations between transit-oriented development and average air quality. It distinguishes rail-based and bus-rapid-transit development, quantity and quality of development, and annual versus seasonal AQI. Its conclusion reports a negative rail association, except for summer average AQI, and no significant BRT association in the spatial model. The statistical design supports an association under its controls; it does not demonstrate that rail investment caused cleaner air or that BRT cannot improve air quality. See manuscript pp. 2 and 19–20.

AQI is already an aggregation: the manuscript defines it from the maximum pollutant-specific index. Averaging it across monitors or periods adds another layer. The authors acknowledge that average AQI can obscure different pollutant relationships and that company/government points of interest proxy for employment density because better employment data were unavailable. Inputs also have different time bases, including 2014 air quality, 2010 population data and transit systems operating by the end of 2015. Those dates must travel with the measurements. See manuscript pp. 6–9 and 19.

The supplied manuscript's sample description needs clarification. It states 152 cities, 116 without either transit system and 37 with one or both. The first two categories total 153. It then lists seven cities with both systems, 14 rail-only and 15 BRT-only, which total 36. These are inconsistencies in the printed description, not permission to alter the data or choose a corrected sample size. The final journal layout and underlying city roster have not been inspected. The source remains a 31-page manuscript rather than the journal's pp. 10–25.

**Application to Assignment 2:** define each quantity before comparing it. A water right, design peak, projected withdrawal, actual consumption and aquifer response answer different questions. A single composite score would hide those differences rather than resolve them. Quantitative comparison should wait for compatible original technical inputs.

## 4. Piracha and Chaudhary: coupled hazards require more than a favorable label

Piracha and Chaudhary (2022) bring urban planning, transport technology, materials, green infrastructure, air pollution and urban heat into one review. The article describes 171 reviewed documents, including academic, policy and media sources, with overlapping thematic categories. It calls itself a systematic review; that description does not turn each cited example into a controlled or pooled causal result. See PDF p. 3, section 1.3.

The useful contribution is the connection among interventions and environmental pathways. Compact development, vehicle changes and heat mitigation may address different parts of the problem. A label such as green infrastructure is therefore an entry into questions about mechanism, location and performance. It is not evidence that all environmental or health effects are favorable. The authors themselves conclude that interactions remain unclear and that empirical research is needed on the effectiveness of planning and mitigation measures. See PDF p. 13, section 6.

**Application to Assignment 2:** retain energy demand, air emissions, noise, wastewater and ecological conditions as potential boundaries of a proposed cooling change when relevant source records warrant them. The review cannot establish that those trade-offs occurred in Cerrillos. It also gives no reason to turn a focused authorization study into a full air-pollution assessment.

## 5. Kalapodis and colleagues: recommendations require a separate implementation test

Kalapodis and colleagues (2025) treat critical infrastructure as both a potential source of wildfire ignition and an asset vulnerable to fire. Their method combines policy review, comparison of institutional arrangements and case synthesis. Two workshops in Spain and Greece involved 13 and 17 participants. Those sessions supplied expert and stakeholder perspectives, not a representative estimate of public preferences. Figures 5 and 6 show the organization of the review rather than a measured causal model. See PDF pp. 10–11.

The paper's institutional comparisons are useful because they distinguish governance tiers, coordination, funding and operational capability. Yet its concluding recommendations remain recommendations. The authors acknowledge regional limits and call for longitudinal research on the effectiveness of implementation. The underlying legislation, project records and case-specific performance claims have not been independently revalidated in this course review. See PDF pp. 32–33.

**Application to Assignment 2:** identify the instrument, issuer, decision scope and evidence of action separately. The existence of a policy response may demonstrate institutional activity; its adequacy and environmental effects require additional evidence. Preserve ordinary regulation or adequate screening as a rival explanation rather than assuming an institutional failure.

## 6. McConnell and colleagues: the measure can change the geography of the problem

The publisher HTML of McConnell and colleagues (2026) was inspected separately from the uploaded PDFs. The article distinguishes occurrence, burned area, destructive events, structures lost, fast-moving fires and fatal events. It argues that the apparent rural or urban concentration of wildfire impacts depends on which impact is measured and which events the dataset includes. This supports a bounded lesson about measurement and coverage; it supplies no Cerrillos result. See the original article's Introduction and Methods at [the publisher](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-026-03336-y).

The article is not counted as an uploaded PDF or as a completed PDF-Translator pack. Local PDF download and page-image retrieval failed. Its code and datasets were located in the article's declarations but were not downloaded or run. No fine-grained figure extraction or replication is claimed.

## Patterns that survive comparison across the readings

The papers converge on the importance of specifying the target of an evaluation, but they do not estimate one common effect. A review's recommendation, a spatial association and a randomized treatment estimate cannot be pooled into a single sustainability score. The useful synthesis is methodological.

First, separate the intervention from the mechanism and the consequence. Blackman and Hoffmann provide the clearest illustration because their measured responses and simulated benefits occupy visibly different parts of the study. Second, preserve the unit of analysis. Chakraborty's tract and development analyses and Gu's city averages make different populations and scales visible. Third, retain the policy-to-implementation distinction. Kalapodis's future-evaluation requirement and Lowe's explicit implementation boundary support it, while leaving open whether a particular institution worked adequately.

Module I supplies the criteria for using these distinctions. North directs attention to rules, enforcement and institutional incentives. Satterthwaite requires attention to costs across boundaries and time. Costanza and Daly separate natural-capital stocks from flows and acknowledge measurement limits. Sen asks which concerns count in evaluation. Ostrom asks when norms and relationships support cooperation rather than assuming a universal mechanism. These are different intellectual tasks, not six interchangeable endorsements of the same framework.

The consequence for Cerrillos is a narrower, stronger memo: examine how specific records treated a proposal and an authorization, state what each source can establish, and leave environmental performance open where the observations are missing.
